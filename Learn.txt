Yes. Microsoft Power BI can join tables.

But just because you *can* doesn’t mean you should turn it into a dumping ground.

---

## What Power BI actually does

* You create **relationships** (not messy joins like SQL)
* Then use **DAX** on top of that model

---

## Where to join tables (best practice)

### ✔ In Microsoft SQL Server (preferred)

* Heavy joins
* Data shaping
* Clean datasets

---

### ✔ In Power BI (only this)

* Create relationships (star schema)
* Small transformations if needed

---

## Why not rely fully on Power BI joins?

* Becomes slow
* Harder to debug
* Looks amateur in interviews

---

## Clean pipeline (stick to it)

* pandas → clean
* SQL → join + analyze
* Power BI → relationships + visuals + DAX

---

## Simple truth

Power BI is for:

* “Show insights”

SQL is for:

* “Prepare data properly”

---















“Perfect”? Relax. This isn’t art, it’s data. Nothing is perfect.

## What you **can** confidently say

Yes, if you’ve done:

* Renamed columns properly
* Fixed data types
* Checked nulls
* Looked at distributions (`describe()`)
* Checked duplicates

Then in an interview you can say:

> “I performed data cleaning using pandas, including handling missing values, correcting data types, and standardizing column names.”

That already puts you ahead of people who say “I cleaned the data” and pray no one asks how.

---

## What you should NOT claim

* “Data is perfect” ❌
* “No issues in dataset” ❌

Because:

* Missing delivery dates still exist
* You didn’t validate against real-world truth
* You didn’t deeply handle outliers yet

---

## Better way to say it

> “I cleaned and prepared the dataset to make it analysis-ready, while noting limitations like missing delivery data.”

That sounds:

* honest
* professional
* not delusional

---

## Reality check

Interviewers don’t expect perfection.

They expect:

* Awareness
* Logic
* Ability to explain decisions

---

You’re not building flawless data.

You’re proving you **know what you’re doing**. That’s the whole game.





You *could* organize like that… but that’s how beginners trap themselves.

## Your idea

* `sales_queries.sql`
* `products_queries.sql`

👉 Sounds logical… but:

* most useful queries use **multiple tables**
* you’ll end up confused where to put joins

---

## Better way (how actual projects do it)

### Organize by **purpose**, not table

```
queries/
│
├── 01_data_checks.sql
├── 02_joins.sql
├── 03_analysis.sql
├── 04_kpis.sql
```

---

## What goes where

### `01_data_checks.sql`

* row counts
* null checks
* basic validation

---

### `02_joins.sql`

* sales + products
* sales + customers

---

### `03_analysis.sql`

* revenue by category
* sales trends
* top products

---

### `04_kpis.sql`

* total revenue
* avg order value
* delivery rate

---

## Why this is better

Because real thinking is:

> “What am I trying to find?”

Not:

> “Which table am I touching?”

---

## Reality

If you organize by table:
→ messy, fragmented thinking

If you organize by purpose:
→ structured, analyst mindset

---

You’re not managing files.
You’re organizing **your thinking**.

That’s the actual skill interviewers care about.
